Every year, we open the call for papers with the same hope: that someone sitting on an interesting idea - maybe a side project, maybe a half-written blog post, maybe a recurring frustration they keep ranting about to coworkers - finally takes the leap and submits. A lot of the best talks we've had at BSidesFrankfurt came from first-time speakers who almost didn't submit because they weren't sure their topic was "big enough." This post is for them.

The 2026 CFP is now open on our Pretalx instance, and it closes on **30 May 2026**. Before you stare at the blank abstract field for the fourth time this week, here's what the review committee actually pays attention to - and what we've learned from reviewing several hundred submissions over the past three editions.

## What we look for

**Technical depth over novelty.** People sometimes assume we only want brand-new research. We don't. A deep, well-structured explanation of a well-understood topic is almost always more useful to an audience than a shallow skim of something novel. If you've spent six months getting genuinely good at, say, reading Windows ETW traces - that is a talk. It doesn't matter that ETW isn't new.

**A clear "so what."** The strongest submissions we receive tell us within the first three sentences of the abstract: what problem the talk addresses, who the audience is, and what they'll leave knowing. We review a lot of abstracts that describe a cool *thing* without ever articulating the *reason* it matters. Make the reader's job easy.

**A sense that you actually did the work.** Reviewers notice the difference between "I read a paper about this" and "I spent three weekends reproducing this and here's what I learned along the way." We want the latter. Show us the scars.

**Calibrated scope.** Forty-five minutes is a surprisingly small amount of time once you subtract intros, demo setup, and Q&A. One clear idea done well beats three ideas done superficially. If you're submitting a 45-minute slot and your abstract mentions six distinct subtopics, that's a signal you haven't found your story yet.

## Common pitfalls

**The vendor pitch in disguise.** BSides is vendor-free by charter. If your abstract mentions a product you sell or a platform you work on, frame the talk around the *technical insight* - not the product. Reviewers can always tell when the demo section is really a sales section.

**The "I'll figure out the details later" abstract.** Abstracts that talk in broad strokes ("we'll cover detection engineering best practices") without specifics almost always get passed over. Concrete examples in the abstract - even one - tell us the talk itself will be concrete.

**Over-claiming.** "Revolutionary," "game-changing," "never-before-seen" - every red flag. Review committees are skeptical by default. Understated claims with strong evidence win. Overstated claims with no evidence lose.

**Ignoring the audience.** BSidesFrankfurt's crowd is a mix: practitioners, researchers, students, and people switching into security. The best talks pitch to a specific subset of that audience and say so up front. "This talk is for SOC analysts with a year or two of experience who want to move into detection engineering" is a much stronger opening than "this talk is for security professionals."

## Sharpening your abstract

Once you have a draft, put it away for 48 hours. Come back, and read only the first two sentences. If a stranger couldn't tell what the talk is about from those two sentences alone, rewrite them. The rest of the abstract is for reviewers who already decided they're interested - but those first two sentences decide whether they get that far.

Then run a brutal edit pass: cut any sentence that doesn't add a fact. "Security is important in 2026" adds no fact. Delete it. "We reverse-engineered three ransomware variants and found a shared implementation flaw in their key-generation routine" adds three facts. Keep it.

Finally, have someone who isn't a security person read it. If they can't tell you, in plain language, what your talk is about, your abstract is still too jargon-heavy for the first pass of our review.

## Practical timing

If you plan to submit, aim to have a draft a full week before the deadline. This gives you time to sit on it, get feedback from one or two people, and resubmit a second version if needed. (Pretalx allows editing submissions until the deadline.) Last-minute submissions aren't penalized, but they correlate strongly with the pitfalls above - probably because nobody had time to read them twice.

We also strongly encourage *first-time* speakers to submit. The committee reserves slots specifically for new voices, and if you've never given a conference talk, please tell us in the notes field - we'll take that into account.

## One last thing

If you're on the fence, submit. The worst-case outcome is a polite decline and feedback from the committee. The best-case outcome is a talk that influences how hundreds of practitioners think about a problem. That asymmetry is why we do this.

See you in Frankfurt.
