When we started planning the first BSidesFrankfurt back in late 2022, the honest answer to "why?" was: because we kept meeting the same people at Munich, Berlin, and Amsterdam, and someone casually said "we could just do one here." The idea sat in a shared document for about six months before any of us took it seriously. Three editions later, with the fourth coming up in September, it feels like a good moment to write down what actually happened and what we've learned.

## The gap we were trying to fill

Frankfurt is a strange security scene. It's one of the largest financial hubs in Europe, which means there's enormous concentration of blue-team, GRC, and financial-crime expertise - but much of that work is confidential, regulated, or simply not the kind of thing people write conference talks about. The city also has a very active hacker community through OpenLab, the local CCC chapter, and university groups at Goethe University and TU Darmstadt - but these communities didn't have a regular technical conference where they could come together.

So the gap was specific: a *technical*, *community-run*, *vendor-free* event that would draw both the regulated-industry practitioners who couldn't easily talk at commercial conferences and the research-leaning community that was already active but scattered across meetups. BSides has always been about removing the barrier to entry, and Frankfurt felt like the right place to do that for this particular mix of people.

## What we got wrong the first year

A lot. Too much to list, but the themes were roughly:

**We massively underestimated catering.** We had food for about 250 people and 340 showed up. Four people stood in a pizza line for an hour. One of them wrote about it afterward. It was fair.

**We over-indexed on talks.** The first edition had four parallel tracks and barely any hallway time. Post-conference feedback was unanimous: fewer tracks, longer breaks, more time to actually talk to people. The second edition collapsed to two tracks and everyone was happier.

**We under-invested in first-time speakers.** We ended up with a strong program, but it was dominated by people who already had a speaking track record. In retrospect, we should have reserved a couple of slots explicitly for first-time speakers from the start. We now do.

**The CFP was confusing.** Our first CFP asked for abstracts, biographies, technical level, track preference, AV requirements, and dietary restrictions in a single 14-field form. People understandably bounced. We've since split submission into two phases: a minimal first-round form, and a second-round follow-up only if the talk is accepted.

## What we're still figuring out

**How international to be.** BSidesFrankfurt has always been English-language, but we debate every year whether to encourage more German-language submissions. The current consensus is to stay English-primary - the community we draw is pan-European and German-only would exclude a lot of Frankfurt's own workforce - but we remain open.

**Capacity.** The first edition had 340 attendees. The second, 420. The third, 470. We could probably fill 600+ this year but we deliberately haven't grown the venue. There's something about a conference where you can credibly bump into every speaker in the hallway that gets lost above a certain size, and we're not ready to lose it.

**Diversity, in every dimension.** We've done OK on gender balance for speakers (roughly 30–35% women and non-binary speakers over the past three editions, which is above the European conference average but not where we want it). We've done less well on experience-level diversity - our audience still skews experienced. We're experimenting with a student rate and direct outreach to university groups to change that.

**Sponsors without compromise.** BSides is built on the principle that sponsors don't get to dictate content. In practice, running a good conference costs real money, and there's constant tension between fundraising and editorial independence. So far we've held the line - no vendor talks, no sponsored sessions, no approval rights over the program - and the sponsors who push back on that generally aren't the sponsors we want long-term.

## Why community conferences still matter in 2026

The commercial conference circuit in Europe has exploded in the last few years. There are more paid events than ever, with bigger budgets and slicker production. That's fine - some of them are genuinely good - but it makes the case for community conferences stronger, not weaker. Community conferences have a different social contract: the organizers are volunteers, the speakers are peers, and the content is chosen on merit by people who will be sitting in the audience. That structure produces a different kind of event, and it's the kind we want to keep producing.

## Looking ahead to 2026

This year we're introducing a track dedicated to defensive engineering - detection, response, and the unglamorous work of keeping production environments boring. We're also adding a CTF for the first time, run in partnership with a local student group. And we're keeping everything that worked: the two-track format, the long hallway breaks, the informal CTF-adjacent evening at a venue we can't disclose until you register, and the stubborn commitment to vendor-free programming.

If you've been before, we hope you'll come back. If you haven't, tickets are on sale now. Either way - thanks for being the reason this exists.
